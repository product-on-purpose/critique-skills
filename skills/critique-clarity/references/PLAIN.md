# PLAIN rubric source - criterion registry

**Status:** Operationalized. Every criterion below carries a paraphrased operationalization, an
operational test, severity 2 and 3 anchors, and a lane assignment with rationale, per
`docs/internal/skill-template.md`'s seven-column format. Zero quotation marks appear in the
Operationalization column, consistent with the paraphrase policy
([ADR 0006](../../../docs/internal/decisions/0006-copyright-paraphrase-policy.md)). Cross-criterion
severity calibration lives in [`severity-anchors.md`](severity-anchors.md).

**Every scripted row names its actual thresholds.** An operational test has to be decidable by two
independent reviewers reaching the same verdict, and a test that says a fixed threshold without
saying which one is not. The numbers below are the constants `scripts/checks.py` implements; the two
are meant to be read together, and a change to either is a change to both.

**Source:** *Federal Plain Language Guidelines*, Plain Language Action and Information Network
(PLAIN), March 2011, Revision 1, May 2011. Canonical URL: https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/
(`rubric_sources.operationalization: open-standard`). Section letters/numbers and page numbers below
are the guidelines document's own (verified against its Table of Contents, pp. iii-v). The Federal
Plain Language Guidelines is an open standard maintained by a US federal interagency working group; no
ISBN applies. Two rows below (PLAIN-NOMINALIZATION, PLAIN-CONCISE) also incorporate Williams & Bizup's
*Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (12th ed.), ch. 3 and ch. 9 respectively; see "Resolution of S-05
OQ-1" below.

## Registry

| ID | Operationalization | Operational test | Severity 2 anchor | Severity 3 anchor | Lane | Lane rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PLAIN-AUDIENCE | A document names or clearly implies a single, specific reader and writes to that reader's existing knowledge; where a document genuinely serves two or more distinct audiences, each audience's content stays in its own section rather than interleaved with the others (PLAIN Sec. I). | Flag a passage as a violation when the artifact addresses two identifiably different readers, for example an applicant and a caseworker, within the same section in a way that makes it unclear which reader a given sentence is for, or when the document assumes background knowledge its stated or implied reader would not have. | A memo written for a general staff audience includes one paragraph of internal jargon clearly aimed at a specialist subset of readers, without flagging that the paragraph is optional for everyone else. | A public-facing benefits notice addresses both the applicant and the applicant's caseworker in the same three paragraphs with no separation, so an applicant reading it cannot tell which instructions are theirs to act on and which describe what the caseworker will do. | judged | Identifying the document's actual intended reader and judging whether two audiences are genuinely distinct requires reading the whole artifact for intent, not a fixed pattern. |
| PLAIN-ORGANIZE | A document's sections and their order follow the sequence of questions or steps the reader actually works through, rather than the order that is easiest for the writer or that mirrors an internal process the reader never sees (PLAIN Sec. II.a). | Flag the document's structure as a violation when a section the reader needs early, such as eligibility or a deadline, appears after sections that only make sense once that earlier information is known, or when the ordering tracks an internal workflow instead of the reader's actual task sequence. | A how-to memo lists a background section before its first actionable step, adding a short detour before the reader can start, but the step order itself is otherwise correct. | An application-instructions document states the submission deadline in its final paragraph, after four pages of eligibility detail, so a reader skimming for the one fact that determines whether to bother reading the rest has to read the entire document first. | judged | Judging whether a given order matches the reader's actual question sequence requires understanding what the reader needs and when, which a fixed pattern cannot infer from section headings alone. |
| PLAIN-HEADINGS | Each section sits under a heading specific enough to be found by scanning, and no heading is left generic enough that a reader cannot tell from it alone whether the section beneath applies to them (PLAIN Sec. II.c-d). | Flag a heading when its text, lowercased and trimmed, is exactly one of the five non-specific labels overview, general, miscellaneous, other, details; that finding is always severity 2. Separately, flag a heading when more than 400 words of paragraph text follow it before the next heading of any level: severity 2 above 400 words, severity 3 above 800. The section threshold sits well above PLAIN-PARAGRAPH's 150-word paragraph threshold on purpose, so one over-long paragraph is reported once, as a paragraph defect, and not a second time as a heading defect. | One section midway through an otherwise well-headed document is titled Other, covering two unrelated minor topics a reader would otherwise find only by reading the whole section. | A twelve-paragraph procedures section runs past 800 words under one heading with no subheading anywhere inside it, so a reader looking for one step has to read the entire section to find out whether the step is in there at all. | scripted | Matching heading text against a fixed non-specific-label list and measuring section length against a named threshold needs no judgment call. |
| PLAIN-ACTIVE | Sentences default to active voice, naming who performs the action, and passive constructions appear only when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or deliberately omitted because the object is the actual point (PLAIN Sec. III.a.1.i). | Flag every sentence carrying a be-verb (am, is, are, was, were, be, been, being), optionally preceded by an auxiliary or modal, immediately followed by a past participle: a word ending in -ed, or one on the fixed irregular-participle list. Severity is 3 when at least three quarters of the sentences in a paragraph of three or more sentences carry the construction, and 2 otherwise. A passive whose actor is genuinely unknown or deliberately suppressed is still detected here and rejected at disposition time; the scripted lane decides the construction, never the actor's relevance, because two reviewers asked to judge relevance would not reliably agree. | A five-sentence paragraph mid-document turns passive for two of its sentences, forcing the reader to re-identify who is doing what, then recovers. | Every step in a five-step setup procedure is written in passive voice, so the reader must guess who performs each action, and the ambiguity compounds across the whole procedure. | scripted | The be-plus-participle construction is a fixed pattern; no judgment call is needed to detect it, and the judgment the guideline's exception needs is deferred to disposition rather than smuggled into the check. |
| PLAIN-NOMINALIZATION | An action described in a sentence is stated as the sentence's own main verb rather than buried inside an abstract noun that then needs a separate, weaker verb, such as make, perform, conduct, or provide, to carry the sentence; the PLAIN guidelines' hidden-verb guidance and Williams' treatment of actions test the identical construction, so this one criterion covers both sources (PLAIN Sec. III.a.1.iii, III.a.2.i; WILLIAMS ch. 3). | Flag a sentence when a generic support verb from the fixed list (make, perform, conduct, provide, give, take, and their inflected forms) is followed within three words by a noun ending in -tion, -sion, -ment, -ance, or -ence. Severity is 3 when at least four fifths of the sentences in a paragraph of three or more sentences carry the pattern, and 2 otherwise. | A single sentence in an otherwise direct paragraph reads the committee conducted an evaluation of the proposal instead of the committee evaluated the proposal, adding a needless extra clause without obscuring meaning. | A five-sentence process paragraph nominalizes the action in every sentence, so the reader has to unpack a weak verb plus a hidden action in every single sentence to follow what actually happens. | scripted | Nominalizing suffixes paired with a fixed list of generic support verbs form a reliable lexical pattern, matching this skill's nominalization-density scripted signal. |
| PLAIN-MUST | A binding requirement is stated using must, never shall or an indirect phrasing that leaves the reader unsure whether an action is optional (PLAIN Sec. III.a.1.iv). | Flag every sentence containing shall, is required to, are required to, or it is necessary that. Severity is 3 only when the document carries two or more such statements and also uses must or should somewhere, so that separate requirements are genuinely stated in competing registers; a document with a single such statement, or one that never uses must or should at all, is severity 2. | A form's instructions use shall once, in an otherwise must-consistent document, in a sentence whose obligation is still clear from context. | A compliance policy alternates between shall, is required to, and must across its five numbered requirements, so a reader cannot tell which of the five are actually mandatory. | scripted | Shall and a fixed list of indirect-requirement phrases are detected by direct lexical match, and the severity escalation counts occurrences rather than weighing how binding a sentence reads. |
| PLAIN-PRONOUNS | The document addresses its reader directly using the second person and refers to the writer's own organization using the first person plural, instead of routing both through third-person institutional substitutes such as the applicant or the Department (PLAIN Sec. III.a.2.ii). | Flag every sentence containing one of the four fixed institutional substitutes: the applicant, the undersigned, this office, the department. Severity is 3 when at least three quarters of the sentences in a paragraph of three or more sentences carry one, and 2 otherwise. | One paragraph describing what happens after submission refers to the applicant three times instead of the reader directly, in a document that otherwise addresses the reader directly. | A five-step instructions section never once addresses the reader directly, instead routing every instruction through the applicant must and the Department will, so the reader has to substitute themselves into every sentence by hand. | scripted | A fixed lexicon of third-person institutional substitutes checked against the absence of second- or first-person address in the same sentence is a direct pattern match, not a judgment call. |
| PLAIN-JARGON | Short, familiar words are used in place of legal, technical, or foreign-language terms wherever a plain equivalent exists, and any technical term the reader genuinely needs is defined at first use (PLAIN Sec. III.a.3.i, III.a.3.v). | Flag a sentence containing one of the four fixed jargon terms (pursuant to, notwithstanding, aforementioned, herein) unless that sentence or the one after it carries a definition marker (meaning, that is, i.e., in other words) or the sentence itself carries a parenthetical gloss. Severity is 3 when three or more sentences in one paragraph qualify, and 2 otherwise. | A single instance of a legal-jargon term appears in an otherwise plain-language paragraph, with the meaning still recoverable from context. | An eligibility section uses three separate legal-jargon terms repeatedly across three consecutive sentences with no plain restatement anywhere nearby, so a reader without legal background cannot determine what the section actually requires. | scripted | A fixed jargon-term list checked against the presence or absence of a nearby definition is a direct lexical match. |
| PLAIN-CONCISE | Words, phrases, and clauses that add length without adding meaning the reader needs are cut; the PLAIN guidelines' word-trimming guidance and Williams' treatment of concision both test the same wordy-phrase pattern, so this one criterion covers both sources (PLAIN Sec. III.a.3.ii; WILLIAMS ch. 9). | Flag a sentence containing one of the four fixed wordy constructions: in order to, due to the fact that, at this point in time, a number of. Severity is 3 when one paragraph carries three or more such constructions in total, and 2 otherwise. | A single sentence uses a wordy stand-in for because instead of because itself, in an otherwise concise paragraph. | A one-paragraph summary packs three separate wordy constructions into two sentences, so the paragraph's actual content is diluted across nearly twice the words it needs. | scripted | A fixed list of wordy phrases with known shorter equivalents is a direct lexical match, mirroring the jargon-list precedent. |
| PLAIN-CONSISTENT-TERMS | One term is used consistently for one concept throughout a document, rather than substituting a synonym for variety, which forces the reader to check whether a new term names something new (PLAIN Sec. III.a.3.iv). | Flag a violation when two or more terms that refer to the identical concept are used interchangeably across the document with no signal, such as a defined-terms section or a stated equivalence, that they are meant to mean the same thing. | A memo uses two different words interchangeably for the same single action in two different sections, without ever using either word for anything else, so a careful reader can work out they mean the same thing but has to check. | A procedure document alternates between three different words for the single artifact a reader is meant to file, so a reader cannot tell whether the three terms name the same document or three different steps. | judged | Deciding whether two terms genuinely refer to the same underlying concept, rather than two related but distinct things, requires reading the document's meaning, not a closed vocabulary match. |
| PLAIN-SENTENCE-LENGTH | Sentences are kept short enough that a reader can hold the whole logical structure in mind on a single pass (PLAIN Sec. III.b.1). | Flag a sentence longer than 30 words, or one carrying more than three subordinate-clause markers from the fixed list (which, that, because, although, though, while, since, unless, whereas, who, whom, whose, if, when). Severity is 3 at 50 words or more, or at four or more markers, and 2 otherwise. | One sentence in an otherwise well-paced paragraph runs to 35 words with a single subordinate clause, longer than ideal but still readable in one pass. | The document's opening paragraph states its main recommendation inside a single sentence of nearly sixty words carrying three subordinate clauses, so a reader cannot state the ask after one pass. | scripted | Word count and subordinate-clause-marker count are directly measurable with no judgment call, matching this skill's sentence-length-distribution scripted signal. |
| PLAIN-SUBJECT-VERB-OBJECT | A sentence's subject, verb, and object are kept close together, rather than separated by a long interrupting clause the reader has to hold open before the sentence resolves (PLAIN Sec. III.b.2). | Split the sentence on commas. When that yields three or more segments and the first segment is eight words or shorter (the subject candidate), the segments between the first and the last are the interrupting asides. Flag the sentence at two or more asides, or when the asides total six or more words. Severity is 3 at two or more asides totalling fifteen or more words, and 2 otherwise. A sentence whose commas form a coordinated series of three or more items belongs to PLAIN-LISTS and is not flagged here. | One sentence inserts a single six-word parenthetical between its subject and verb, a minor interruption in an otherwise direct paragraph. | A policy sentence's subject is followed by a twenty-plus-word interrupting clause with two nested commas before the verb finally appears, so the reader has to hold the subject in mind through an unrelated aside before learning what it does. | scripted | Counting words and comma-set-off clauses between a sentence's subject and its next verb is a fixed, reproducible heuristic that needs no interpretive judgment. |
| PLAIN-DOUBLE-NEGATIVE | Double negatives and stacked exceptions-to-exceptions are avoided, so the reader is not forced to work out a statement's logical sign before understanding it; a negated adjective counts only when it is on the closed marker list, because a bare un- or in- prefix is not reliably negating (PLAIN Sec. III.b.3). | Count negation markers from the closed list (not, no, never, none, neither, nor, nothing, nobody, cannot and the common contracted forms, without, unless, except, excluding, ineligible, invalid, unable, unavailable, noncompliant) within one sentence. Flag the sentence at two or more markers; severity is 3 at three or more, and 2 otherwise. The unit is the sentence, not the clause: a reliable clause boundary needs a parser this lane does not have, and a sentence is the widest unit two reviewers can segment identically. | A single sentence combines two mild negation markers in one clause, still resolvable on one reading. | An eligibility rule stacks three negations and an exception-to-an-exception in one sentence, so the reader must work out the statement's logical sign in stages before understanding what it actually permits. | scripted | Counting negation markers from a fixed set within a clause boundary is a direct pattern match. |
| PLAIN-MAIN-IDEA-FIRST | The main rule or idea is stated before its exceptions and conditions, not after, so the reader is not left holding an unresolved statement while conditions accumulate (PLAIN Sec. III.b.4). | Flag a passage as a violation when a sentence or paragraph opens with a condition, exception, or qualifier and only states the governing rule after it, in a way that requires re-reading to recover the rule. | One sentence opens with a single short qualifying clause before stating its rule, a minor inversion that does not require re-reading. | A benefits rule opens with three sentences of exceptions and conditions before the fourth sentence finally states what the benefit actually is, so a reader has to reach the end before the opening sentences make sense. | judged | Identifying which clause is the governing rule and which are exceptions requires reading the passage's meaning, not a fixed lexical pattern. |
| PLAIN-PARAGRAPH | Each paragraph opens with a topic sentence, covers exactly one topic, and stays short enough to scan (PLAIN Sec. III.c.1, III.c.3-4). | In a paragraph of three or more sentences, take the content words of the first sentence and of the rest of the paragraph (lowercased, with a fixed stopword list and every word of two letters or fewer removed) and flag the paragraph at severity 3 when fewer than 15 percent of the first sentence's content words recur anywhere in the rest. Otherwise flag the paragraph at severity 2 when it runs past 150 words. The two branches are exclusive: a paragraph is reported once, under whichever branch fires first. | One paragraph runs slightly over the length threshold but its first sentence still clearly announces the single topic that follows. | A six-sentence paragraph opens with a sentence about scheduling, then spends its remaining five sentences on eligibility criteria unrelated to scheduling, so the opening sentence gives the reader no useful preview of what follows. | scripted | Paragraph length and first-sentence-to-body word overlap are both directly measurable with a fixed formula, needing no interpretive judgment. |
| PLAIN-TRANSITIONS | Transition words are used to tell the reader how a new sentence or paragraph relates to what came before it (PLAIN Sec. III.c.2). | Walk the paragraphs in document order. A paragraph that is the first in its section is exempt, since it opens a topic rather than continuing one. Flag any other paragraph that does not begin with one of the fixed transition words or phrases (however, therefore, in addition, as a result, moreover, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, meanwhile, similarly, in contrast, for example, for instance, in fact, indeed, likewise, thus, hence, accordingly, otherwise, additionally, also, then, next, finally, in summary). Severity is 3 for every paragraph in an unbroken run of four or more flagged paragraphs, and 2 otherwise. | One paragraph transition in an otherwise well-connected section has no transition word, but the relationship to the prior paragraph is still recoverable from content alone. | Four consecutive paragraphs in a policy explanation carry no transition words at all, so the reader cannot tell whether each new paragraph adds a supporting point, states an exception, or contradicts the one before it. | scripted | Checking for a fixed set of connector words at each paragraph boundary is a direct lexical match. |
| PLAIN-LISTS | Sequential steps, parallel options, or comparable attributes are presented as a list or table rather than folded into a run-on sentence or paragraph (PLAIN Sec. III.d.2-3). | Normalize the sentence's coordinated series: replace each and or or that joins two segments with a comma boundary, then split on commas and semicolons. Flag the sentence when that yields three or more items, or when it carries three or more ordinal step markers (first, second, third, fourth, fifth, each followed immediately by a comma). Severity is 3 at five or more items or markers, and 2 otherwise. A sentence carrying no and or or coordinator anywhere is not a series, however many commas it has; that is PLAIN-SUBJECT-VERB-OBJECT's case. | One sentence embeds three short parallel options inline with commas, readable but better suited to a list. | A five-step setup procedure is written as one paragraph using inline step markers for each step, so the reader has to parse a single long sentence to extract a sequence a formatted list would show at a glance. | scripted | Counting comma- or semicolon-separated parallel items and step markers within a single sentence is a fixed, reproducible pattern. |

## Sections surveyed and not carried forward

Sec. IV ("Write for the web") and Sec. V ("Test") were read and excluded: Sec. IV is channel-specific
guidance (links, PDFs, web page structure) that does not map onto this skill's v0.1 artifact claim
(markdown/plain-text prose documents, not web pages as such), and Sec. V describes a testing process
(paraphrase testing, usability testing with real readers), which evaluates a method, not an inspectable
artifact, so it fails the methodology's Part 1 gate test outright (methodology.md Sec. 2). Sec.
III.a.1.ii ("Use the simplest form of a verb") and III.a.1.v ("Use contractions when appropriate") were
surveyed and folded into PLAIN-CONCISE and PLAIN-JARGON respectively rather than kept as standalone
IDs; both are narrow restatements of "prefer the shorter, plainer option" already covered there.

### No aggregate readability-grade criterion

[S-05 (skills-slate spec)](../../../docs/internal/release-plans/plan_v0.1.0/S-05_skills-slate/spec.md)'s
per-skill table names a readability grade first among clarity's expected scripted signals, and
[methodology section 7](../../../docs/explanation/methodology.md#7-determinism-model) uses readability
grades as its own example of something the scripted lane can compute. No `PLAIN-READABILITY` criterion
ships here, and that is a decision rather than an omission.

A grade-level formula such as Flesch-Kincaid is a *computation*, not a *criterion*. The Federal Plain
Language Guidelines nowhere state a numeric grade a document must reach; the closest material, Sec. V,
describes testing a draft with real readers, which fails Part 1 of the methodology's gate for the
reason recorded above. A criterion that set a grade threshold would therefore be the skill author's
own judgment wearing a citation, which is precisely what Part 2 of the gate exists to reject
(methodology section 2; section 9, "Not taste"). The guidelines' readability guidance reaches this
registry through the criteria that do trace to stated sections instead: PLAIN-SENTENCE-LENGTH
(Sec. III.b.1), PLAIN-JARGON (Sec. III.a.3.i, III.a.3.v), PLAIN-NOMINALIZATION (Sec. III.a.1.iii,
III.a.2.i), and PLAIN-CONCISE (Sec. III.a.3.ii), which between them measure the sentence length and
word choice any grade formula is a weighted sum of.

A grade score remains perfectly reasonable as a *reported metric* alongside the findings, which is how
methodology section 7 uses the term. It is out of scope for v0.1 because nothing in this skill's
output surface carries metrics that are not findings.

## Resolution of S-05 OQ-1

[ADR 0019](../../../docs/internal/decisions/0019-clarity-two-namespaces-merged-duplicate-criteria.md)
resolves S-05 (skills-slate spec) OQ-1: this skill keeps two namespaces, PLAIN and WILLIAMS, rather
than folding WILLIAMS-* entirely into PLAIN-*, because six of Williams' eight candidate criteria
(character-as-subject, cohesion, coherence, stress, sentence shape, parallelism) have no PLAIN
equivalent and would be misattributed to an open-standard source if renamed into it. The two criterion
pairs that tested the literal same construction across both sources merge into one ID each, owned in
this file: PLAIN-NOMINALIZATION absorbs Williams ch. 3's treatment of actions, and PLAIN-CONCISE
absorbs Williams ch. 9's treatment of concision. WILLIAMS-NOMINALIZATION and WILLIAMS-CONCISION were
never published as separate IDs. See `WILLIAMS.md` for the remaining six WILLIAMS-only criteria and the
same resolution note from that source's side.
